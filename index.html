📈 Aufbau Principle — Filling Order

Aufbau Principle

Electrons fill orbitals starting from the lowest energy level. Combined with the (n+l) rule, the actual filling order deviates from simple n ordering.

↑↓ Hund's Rule & Pauli Principle

Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity

In degenerate orbitals, electrons occupy each orbital singly before pairing up, with parallel spins (same ms).

Pauli Exclusion Principle

No two electrons in an atom can have identical values of all four quantum numbers. Each orbital holds maximum 2 electrons with opposite spins.

📉 Periodic Trends

Atomic Radius

↓ Increases down a group (more shells)
← Decreases across a period (more protons pull electrons closer)

Ionization Energy

↑ Increases across a period (harder to remove electrons from more nuclear charge)
↓ Decreases down group

Electronegativity

Highest at F (3.98). Increases across periods, decreases down groups. Related to both IE and EA.

Electron Affinity

Energy released when an atom gains an electron. Generally increases across a period; halogens have highest EA.

⚡ Energy Levels & Nodes
📖 Orbital Types — Complete Reference
s orbitals
l = 0, spherically symmetric, 1 orientation (ml = 0). Node count = n−1 radial nodes. Highest probability near nucleus. Penetrates well, stabilized by nuclear attraction. Each shell has one s orbital.
p orbitals
l = 1, dumbbell-shaped with node at nucleus, 3 orientations (px, py, pz). Start from n=2. Radial nodes = n−2. Participate in π-bonding when sideways overlap occurs.
d orbitals
l = 2, 5 orientations with various lobes and toruses. Start from n=3. Essential in transition metal chemistry, crystal field theory. dz² has unique torus shape.
f orbitals
l = 3, 7 complex orientations. Start from n=4. Found in lanthanides/actinides. Poorly shielded, responsible for lanthanide contraction and unusual properties.
📊 Radial Distribution Functions

Radial Distribution Function (RDF)

The RDF 4πr²|ψ|² gives the probability of finding an electron in a shell of radius r. Peaks of the RDF correspond to the most probable radial distances. The number of peaks equals n−l. Key insight: 2s penetrates inside 1s shells, explaining why 2s has lower energy than 2p despite same n.

⚡ Ionic Bond — 3D Lattice
📚 Theory

Ionic Bonding

Formed by complete electron transfer from metal to non-metal. The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (cations and anions) constitutes the ionic bond.

Lattice Energy (U)

Born-Landé Equation: U = −(NAMz⁺z⁻e²)/(4πε₀r₀) × (1−1/n)
Lattice energy determines stability, melting point, and solubility. Higher charge → higher lattice energy.

Born-Haber Cycle

Thermodynamic cycle using Hess's Law to calculate lattice energy indirectly from enthalpy of formation, atomization, ionization, and electron affinity.

🔗 Covalent Bond — Sigma & Pi
📚 Bond Types

Sigma (σ) Bond

Head-on (end-to-end) overlap of orbitals along the internuclear axis. Cylindrically symmetrical. Allows free rotation. All single bonds are σ. Stronger than π bonds.

Pi (π) Bond

Lateral (sideways) overlap of p-orbitals above and below the internuclear axis. No free rotation. Double bonds = 1σ + 1π; Triple = 1σ + 2π.

Bond Parameters

Bond Length: Distance at minimum potential energy
Bond Energy: Energy to break 1 mole of bonds
Bond Order: (Bonding−Antibonding)/2
Higher bond order → shorter, stronger bond

⚙ Metallic Bonding — Electron Sea
📚 Metallic Bonding Theory

Electron Sea Model

Metal cations arranged in a lattice, surrounded by a "sea" of delocalized free electrons. These mobile electrons account for electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, and metallic lustre.

Band Theory

In a metal crystal, atomic orbitals combine to form continuous bands. The partially filled conduction band allows free electron movement. Insulators have full valence bands with large bandgaps; semiconductors have small bandgaps.

Properties Explained

• Malleability/Ductility: Layers slide without breaking bonds (non-directional).
• High MP: Strong delocalized bonding
• Conductivity: Free electron movement
• Lustre: Free electrons interact with light

Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

A covalent bond forms when half-filled atomic orbitals of two atoms overlap. The greater the overlap, the stronger the bond. The orbitals must have compatible symmetry and similar energies. VBT explains bond directionality and stereochemistry through hybridization.

🔀 Overlap Types

s-s overlap

H₂ molecule: 1s-1s head-on overlap. Pure σ-bond.

s-p overlap

HF: H 1s with F 2pz. Head-on, σ-bond.

p-p overlap

Head-on: σ-bond (F₂, Cl₂). Sideways: π-bond (O₂, N₂).

d orbital overlap

p-d (expanded octets), d-d bonding in transition metal compounds.

Coordinate Bond

A covalent bond where both electrons come from the same atom (the donor/ligand). The acceptor provides the empty orbital. Once formed, it is indistinguishable from a regular covalent bond. Also called a dative bond.

Lewis Acid-Base Concept

Lewis base (donor): Provides lone pair — e.g., NH₃, H₂O, Cl⁻
Lewis acid (acceptor): Accepts lone pair — e.g., BF₃, AlCl₃, metal ions

💧 Hydrogen Bonding
📚 H-Bond Theory

Hydrogen Bond

Electrostatic attraction between a partially positive H atom (bonded to F, O, or N) and the lone pair of an electronegative atom (F, O, N) in the same or neighbouring molecule. Strength: 10−40 kJ/mol.

Types of H-bonds

Intermolecular: Between different molecules (water, HF, NH₃). Responsible for high boiling points and surface tension.
Intramolecular: Within the same molecule (e.g., salicylaldehyde, 2-nitrophenol). Reduces boiling point.

Anomalous Properties of Water

• High boiling point (100°C vs expected −80°C)
• High surface tension and viscosity
• Ice is less dense than liquid water
• High heat capacity (4.18 J/g·K)

〰 Van der Waals Forces

London Dispersion Forces

Temporary induced dipole-induced dipole interactions. Present in ALL molecules. Strongest in large, polarizable molecules (noble gases, alkanes). Increases with molecular size and surface area.

Dipole-Dipole

Between polar molecules with permanent dipoles. Molecules align partially positive ends with partially negative ends. Strength depends on magnitude of dipole moment (μ = qd).

Ion-Dipole

Between ions and polar molecules. Important in dissolution of ionic compounds. Hydration energy from ion-dipole interactions must exceed lattice energy for dissolution to occur.

Relative Strengths (Intermolecular vs Intramolecular)

Covalent bonds (~200−1000 kJ/mol) >> H-bonds (10−40 kJ/mol) > Dipole-dipole (5−20 kJ/mol) > London dispersion (0.05−40 kJ/mol). All intermolecular forces are weaker than covalent/ionic bonds, which explains why gases have easily separable molecules while crystals require more energy to break apart.

📐 VSEPR Theory
Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion — predicting molecular geometry

VSEPR Principle

Electron pairs (both bonding pairs BP and lone pairs LP) around a central atom arrange themselves to minimize repulsion. Lone pair−lone pair repulsion > lone pair−bonding pair > bonding pair−bonding pair. This drives the geometry away from idealized angles.

🌐 Molecular Orbital Theory
Linear combination of atomic orbitals, MO diagrams, bond order & magnetism

Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)

In MOT, atomic orbitals combine (LCAO — Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals) to form molecular orbitals that are delocalized over the entire molecule. Combination of n atomic orbitals gives n molecular orbitals: n/2 bonding (lower energy, stabilizing) and n/2 antibonding (higher energy, destabilizing). Bond Order = (Bonding electrons − Antibonding electrons) / 2.

📐 LCAO Method

Linear Combination

ψ(MO) = c₁φ₁ ± c₂φ₂

Bonding MO: ψb = c₁φ₁ + c₂φ₂ (constructive interference)
Antibonding MO: ψ*a = c₁φ₁ − c₂φ₂ (destructive interference)

Conditions for LCAO

1. Similar energy of combining AOs
2. Same symmetry about bonding axis
3. Significant overlap of AOs

⚡ Bond Order & Stability

Bond Order Formula

BO = (Nb − Na) / 2

Where Nb = electrons in bonding MOs
Na = electrons in antibonding MOs

BO > 0 → stable molecule
BO = 0 → unstable (He₂, Ne₂)

Relationship

Higher BO → shorter bond length, higher bond energy, higher dissociation energy

🧲 Magnetic Properties

Diamagnetic

All electrons paired. No net magnetic moment. Slightly repelled by magnetic field.
Examples: N₂, F₂, H₂, CO

Paramagnetic

Unpaired electrons present. Net magnetic moment. Attracted to magnetic field.
Examples: O₂ (2 unpaired), B₂, NO (1 unpaired)

MOT predicts O₂ is paramagnetic!

VBT incorrectly predicts O₂ as diamagnetic. MOT correctly shows 2 unpaired electrons in degenerate π* 2p orbitals — a triumph of MOT.

📈 MO Energy Ordering for 2nd Period Diatomics
For Z ≤ 7 (Li₂ to N₂) — s-p mixing significant:
σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < π2p = π2p < σ2p < π*2p = π*2p < σ*2p
For Z ≥ 8 (O₂ to Ne₂) — s-p mixing less significant:
σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < σ2p < π2p = π2p < π*2p = π*2p < σ*2p

📖 Key Concepts Glossary

Aufbau Principle

Electrons fill lowest energy orbitals first. Order: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p... Determined by (n+l) rule.

Pauli Exclusion

Max 2 electrons per orbital, with opposite spins. Consequence: shells have 2n² electrons maximum.

Hund's Rule

Degenerate orbitals fill singly before pairing. Parallel spins in half-filled state → maximum multiplicity → extra stability.

Shielding Effect

Inner electrons reduce nuclear attraction felt by outer electrons. Effective nuclear charge Z* = Z − σ (screening constant).

Penetration

s > p > d > f penetration of inner shells. s electrons closer to nucleus are more stabilized. Explains why 3d fills after 4s.

Electron Affinity

Energy released on gaining an electron. Halogens have highest EA. O and S have anomalously low EA due to electron-electron repulsion in compact orbitals.

Ionisation Energy

Energy to remove electron from gaseous atom. Anomalies: Be > B (2s vs 2p), N > O (half-filled 2p stability).

Polarisability

Ease of distorting electron cloud. Increases with size, decreases with charge. Governs London dispersion force strength.

Fajan's Rules

Ionic compounds gain covalent character when: small highly-charged cation, large highly-charged anion. Covalent character distorts anion electron cloud (polarisation).
